A digital museum of Nigeria's creative history Photography · Music · Fashion · Design

Nigeria's creative history is not a gap in the record. It is a record that has been held elsewhere, catalogued under someone else's name, and made hard to reach.

ARCHIVE is a reading of that history across four disciplines — what was made, who made it, when, and who holds it now. Every claim on this page carries its source. Where a work cannot be shown, the page names it anyway: the title, the date, the institution, the accession number and the rights status, in the place where the image would have been.

Do not merely display the past. Make people feel its influence on the present.

04 Explore the disciplines

Four disciplines, four registers

They are not equally documented, and this page does not pretend otherwise. Photography and Music carry depth. Fashion rests on one well-documented designer and strong object records. Design is thin, and says so.

01 · Photography

Photo­graphy

10 sourced entries · institutional holdings verified

1856 · Abeokuta and Lagos

Daniel West, a Methodist pastor from Britain, apparently took daguerreotypes in Abeokuta and Lagos — but only engravings survive. ◐

1880 onwards · Lagos

Professional photographic studios were established in Lagos, often run by Sierra Leoneans, Liberians or Brazilians in Nigeria. Neils Walwin Holm was, from the 1890s until 1910, “the pre-eminent photographer of Lagos.” ◐

1968 onwards

J.D. 'Okhai Ojeikere began documenting Nigerian hairstyles, printing roughly a thousand pictures and leaving an archive of well over 10,000 photographs. ●

02 · Music

Music

12 sourced entries · one corrected common myth

19th century onwards

Highlife is Ghanaian. It originated along the coastal cities of present-day Ghana and spread to Nigeria after the Second World War as Igbo highlife, which became Nigeria's most popular genre in the 1960s. ◐

1920s · Lagos

Jùjú, a Yoruba popular music, began developing in Lagos with Tunde King. Talking drums were not introduced to the genre until 1948. ◐

1960s onward vs 2000s–2010s

Afrobeat and Afrobeats are not the same genre. Afrobeat fuses Yoruba and Ghanaian music with American funk, jazz and soul, pioneered in the 1960s by Fela Kuti with drummer Tony Allen. Afrobeats is an umbrella term for popular music developed in Nigeria, Ghana and the UK in the 2000s and 2010s. ●

03 · Fashion

Fashion

8 sourced entries · object-level records at the V&A

1960 · Yaba, Lagos

Shade Thomas-Fahm opened Maison Shadé and popularised the use of hand-dyed adire and aso oke in ready-to-wear. The V&A heads its own trail “Nigeria's first fashion designer: Shade Thomas-Fahm” — a claim made by the museum, so this page reports it as theirs. ○

20th century · Abeokuta and Ibadan

Adire is resist-dyed cloth made by Yoruba women — from adi (“to tie”) and re (“to soak”). Imports of shirting material in the early 20th century caused a boom that made it a major local craft. ◐

19th century onwards

Ankara is not a traditional Nigerian cloth. It is industrially produced cotton with batik-inspired printing, introduced to West Africans by Dutch merchants in the 19th century from Indonesian batik. The name comes from the Hausa word for Accra. ◐

04 · Design

Design

9 sourced entries · the thinnest of the four. Stated, not hidden.

1958–1962 · Zaria

The Zaria Art Society was formed by students who rejected the Western-focused curriculum of their mainly British lecturers and promoted local traditions such as Nok sculpture and Uli painting. The style is now called natural synthesis — a term coined by Uche Okeke. ◐

1961 onwards · Ibadan

The Mbari Club was founded by Ulli Beier with Wole Soyinka and Chinua Achebe, who suggested the name. It published 17 titles by African writers in the 1960s, and Fela Kuti made his debut as a bandleader there. ◐

The gap

Nigerian graphic design proper — posters, book jackets, record sleeves, signage, type — could not be sourced. What stands in for it here is art, publishing and architecture. ⊘

06 Featured creator

J.D. 'Okhai Ojeikere

1930–2014. The best-documented subject in this archive — and the reason this page is built the way it is.

Johnson Donatus Aihumekeokhai Ojeikere was a Nigerian photographer. In 1968 he began documenting Nigerian hairstyles, printing approximately a thousand pictures of African women's hair and leaving behind an archive of well over 10,000 photographs.

His work is now held by four of the institutions this project relies on. That is the good news. It is also the problem: not one of those holdings is ours to reproduce. Every one of them is recorded as not public domain, and MoMA's own page routes reproduction through a licensing process.

So we name the work instead. The title, the date, the accession, the institution — and, in the space where the photograph would be, the reason you cannot see it.

This is not a workaround. It is the argument: that Nigeria's creative history is real, documented, and largely held elsewhere — and that saying so plainly is more honest than a page of borrowed images with no provenance.

Institutional holdings 4 collections

MoMA 3 works online — Fro (1970), Two in One Piko (1971), Brush Eko Bridge (1973). Reproduction is handled through a licensing process.
Met 2 works — Untitled (Modern Suku) (1975) and Untitled (Mkpuk Eba) (1974), accessioned 2010. Recorded not public domain.
AIC Ife Bronze, from the series “Hairstyles” (1972). Recorded not public domain.

07 Closing

What we could not verify

Preservation is not the same as presentation. A record that cannot say what it does not know is not a record — it is a brochure.

Kept off the page, on purpose

These are the things this project was told to look for and could not source. They are listed here rather than quietly dropped, and none of them appears above as a fact.

  • Nigerian record sleeve design. No source reachable here documents it as a history. Plate 06 marks the hole.
  • Nigerian poster practice and FESTAC '77 poster work. The emblem is verified; the posters are not.
  • “The Lagos studio portrait tradition” as a named genre. Lagos studios from 1880 are sourced; the tradition as a documented category is not.
  • FESTAC '77 as a photography documentation project. The festival is well sourced; a photographic project around it is not.
  • Individual contemporary Afrobeats artists. The genre term and its timeline are sourced. No artist was verified, so none is named.
  • Akinola Lasekan; Don Barber; Amos Olarenwaju Osidele. Named in project notes, not sourced. Dropped.

Corrections this page makes

Several widely repeated claims turned out to be wrong or contested. Each is corrected in place above rather than repeated.

  • Highlife is Ghanaian in origin. Igbo highlife is the Nigerian form.
  • Afrobeat is not Afrobeats — and Fela did not invent it alone. Fela himself said: “without Tony Allen, there would be no Afrobeat.”
  • Ankara is not traditional Nigerian cloth. It is Dutch-introduced, batik-derived, and named after Accra.
  • Adire means adi + re — “to tie” + “to soak”, not the generic “tie and dye”.
  • Ojeikere's hairstyles are dated 1968, not the 1950s. The source page contradicts itself; 1968 is the dated statement.
  • “First Nigerian fashion designer” is the V&A's claim, so it is attributed to the V&A.

A note on the images

There are no photographs on this page. Not because none exist — the opposite — but because almost every significant work in this history is held by an institution that has not licensed it for reproduction, and inventing substitutes would be a lie told in the exact place where trust is being asked for.

The plates name what belongs there. That is the promise the rest of this archive is built on.
